The Question

Act I

What if people ignore the treatment they were assigned?

A lottery or a coin flip decides who should be treated — that is the clean experiment of Chapter 1.

But police officers override their orders, patients skip their pills, and lottery winners never enroll. What is left of the experiment?

Open with the gap between the plan and reality. In Chapter 1 we assumed everyone did what the lottery said. Real experiments rarely go to plan: officers use discretion, patients stop taking medication, families who win a school seat decide not to take it. Ask the room: if half the treatment group never got treated, can we still learn the effect of the treatment?

Comparing people by the treatment they received brings selection bias back

Whoever decides to deviate from the plan does so for a reason — and that reason may also drive the outcome.

If officers arrest the most aggressive suspects regardless of what the random form said, then the “arrested” group is loaded with high-risk cases. Comparing outcomes by delivered treatment mixes the effect of arrest with the danger of the incident — the same selection-bias problem from Chapter 1, now sneaking in through non-compliance.

Our route through the chapter

  • The question — why non-compliance breaks a simple comparison of treated and untreated
  • The investigation — the IV chain reaction, the three conditions for a valid instrument, who LATE speaks for, and three case studies: police responses, a charter-school lottery, family size
  • The resolution — 2SLS in practice, weak instruments, and the traps to avoid

Learning objectives: explain why non-compliance creates a gap between assigned and received treatment; define LATE and the formula LATE = reduced form / first stage; classify compliers, always-takers, never-takers and defiers; state the three requirements for a valid instrument; explain how 2SLS implements IV; recognize weak instruments.

Every IV analysis has three variables

Instrument \(Z\)

The random nudge — a lottery win, an assignment form.

Treatment \(D\)

What people actually received — the action taken, the school attended.

Outcome \(Y\)

What we care about — repeat violence, test scores, years of schooling.

IV uses the randomness in \(Z\) to learn how \(D\) affects \(Y\).

Remote-control analogy: the remote (instrument) does not entertain you; it only changes the channel (treatment), which determines what you watch (outcome). Throughout the chapter, Z is assignment, D is the treatment received, Y is the outcome.

Three measurements and four kinds of people carry the chapter

The measurements

  • First stage — how much \(Z\) moves \(D\)
  • Reduced form — how much \(Z\) moves \(Y\)
  • LATE — the effect of \(D\) on \(Y\) for those \(Z\) moves

The people

  • Compliers — follow the nudge
  • Always-takers — treated no matter what
  • Never-takers — untreated no matter what
  • Defiers — do the opposite

The method is built from the left column; its interpretation depends on the right.

Other vocabulary we will meet: non-compliance, relevance, independence, exclusion restriction, monotonicity, two-stage least squares (2SLS), weak instruments. Define them as they come up.

The Investigation

Act II

Assigned treatment and received treatment are two different variables

Assigned (\(Z\))

  • Decided by the researcher’s lottery
  • Random by design
  • Like a doctor’s prescription

Received (\(D\))

  • Decided by people on the ground
  • Can depend on anything — including risk
  • Like the pill actually swallowed

Non-compliance is the gap between the two columns — and only the left one is guaranteed random.

Some patients never fill the prescription; others get the pill from a friend. The prescription and the pill are different things. Randomization protects the prescription, not the pill.

Minneapolis police were randomly told to arrest, advise, or separate

Assigned response What the officer should do Cases
Arrest Take the suspect into custody 92
Advise Counsel or mediate the couple 108
Separate Send the suspect away for 8 hours 114

The goal: learn which response best prevents future domestic violence.

The Minneapolis Domestic Violence Experiment (MDVE), early 1980s. Officers responding to misdemeanor domestic-violence calls followed a randomly ordered set of instructions. 314 cases in the clean data file (mdve_clean.csv), with two columns: assigned and delivered.

Officers obeyed arrest orders almost always, and the others far less

Form said Arrest Advise Separate Obeyed
Arrest 91 0 1 98.9%
Advise 19 84 5 77.8%
Separate 26 5 83 72.8%

Rows: the randomly assigned response · columns: what the officer actually did.

When officers deviated, they almost always deviated toward arrest.

Read the diagonal: 91 of 92 arrest assignments were carried out, versus 84 of 108 advise and 83 of 114 separate. The off-diagonal mass sits in the Arrest column: 19 + 26 = 45 coddle assignments ended in arrest. The “arrested” group therefore contains the randomly assigned arrests plus the cases officers judged most dangerous — a recipe for selection bias.

IV works like a chain of dominoes

We see the first domino fall and the last one fall; IV infers the push in between.

Domino 1: the random form nudges the officer’s action (first stage). Domino 2: the action affects future violence (the causal effect we want). What we can measure directly is the form’s effect on violence (reduced form) = domino 1 × domino 2. The IV trick divides out domino 1.

First stage and reduced form are two simple comparisons by \(Z\)

\[\phi = E[D_i \mid Z_i = 1] - E[D_i \mid Z_i = 0]\]

\[\rho = E[Y_i \mid Z_i = 1] - E[Y_i \mid Z_i = 0]\]

\(\phi\): how far the nudge moves treatment. \(\rho\): how far it moves the outcome. Both are clean, because \(Z\) is random.

Both comparisons inherit the experiment’s randomization — neither uses the treatment people chose.

φ (phi) is the first stage; ρ (rho) is the reduced form, also called the intent-to-treat (ITT) effect. Each is just a difference in averages between the Z = 1 and Z = 0 groups, exactly like the Chapter 1 comparisons.

Dividing the reduced form by the first stage isolates the treatment effect

\[\lambda_{LATE} = \frac{\rho}{\phi} = \frac{\text{effect of } Z \text{ on } Y}{\text{effect of } Z \text{ on } D}\]

If the nudge moves only part of the group, its effect on \(Y\) is diluted by the same fraction — so we scale it back up.

LATE = reduced form ÷ first stage: the Wald ratio.

Intuition: if winning a lottery raises scores by 0.3 but only 60% of winners actually attend, the effect of attending must be about 0.3 / 0.6 = 0.5 for those who attend because they won. This ratio is the Wald estimator, named after statistician Abraham Wald.

The idea dates back to 1928, to untangle supply from demand

  • Philip G. Wright, in an appendix to a book on tariffs, used outside factors that shift one curve but not the other
  • His son Sewall Wright, a geneticist, supplied the path-diagram mathematics
  • The idea then lay largely dormant for decades

Instruments are “external factors” that move one part of a system while leaving the rest alone.

Price and quantity are set jointly by supply and demand, so a regression of quantity on price estimates neither curve. Wright’s insight: a factor that shifts supply (e.g., weather affecting harvests) but not demand traces out the demand curve. Modern IV is the same idea applied to experiments with non-compliance and to natural experiments.

Relevance and independence can be checked in the data

Relevance

  • \(Z\) must actually move \(D\)
  • Check: a strong first stage
  • MDVE: the form moved actual coddling a lot

Independence

  • \(Z\) must be as good as randomly assigned
  • Check: balance across \(Z\) groups
  • MDVE, KIPP: a lottery guarantees it by design

Two of the three conditions leave fingerprints we can inspect.

Key analogy: an instrument that does not move treatment is a key that does not turn the lock. Independence means the instrument is unrelated to unobserved confounders — a coin flip does not know which player is better. With natural experiments (not lotteries), independence needs a careful argument plus balance checks.

Exclusion: the instrument may reach the outcome only through the treatment

No data test can rule out a side channel — exclusion must be argued, not proven.

If simply winning the lottery energized parents, who then helped with homework, the lottery would affect scores directly and the IV estimate would be contaminated. Placebo analogy: if you feel better from the ritual of swallowing a pill, the pill’s chemistry is not the only channel. This is the hardest condition to defend in any IV study.

An instrument sorts people into four types

Type If \(Z = 0\) If \(Z = 1\) What IV learns about them
Complier untreated treated Their effect = LATE
Always-taker treated treated Nothing (unmoved)
Never-taker untreated untreated Nothing (unmoved)
Defier treated untreated Ruled out by assumption

Only people whose treatment responds to the instrument contribute to the IV estimate.

Restaurant analogy: a complier orders the daily special, so changing the special changes their meal; an always-taker orders pizza whatever the menu says. We can never label an individual — we only see one value of Z per person — but we can estimate the share of each type from the data.

Monotonicity rules out defiers, and MDVE crossovers run one way

Crossover direction Cases Rate
Told to coddle → arrested 45 of 222 20.3%
Told to arrest → coddled 1 of 92 1.1%

Officers bent the rules toward arrest, almost never away from it — consistent with no defiers.

Monotonicity: the instrument pushes everyone in the same direction or not at all. A defier would be an officer who coddles when told to arrest and arrests when told to coddle. Monotonicity is about counterfactual behavior, so it cannot be formally tested, but this lopsided pattern makes it plausible. Without it, complier and defier effects could cancel in the first stage.

LATE speaks for the compliers and stays silent about everyone else

  • The “L” in LATE means local — local to the people the instrument moves
  • Always-takers and never-takers may have very different effects
  • A different instrument moves a different group, so it can give a different LATE

Before generalizing an IV estimate, ask: who are the compliers here?

In the MDVE, compliers are cases where the officer did what the form said. Always-takers here are officers who arrest no matter what — possibly more experienced, and arrest may work differently in their cases. A KIPP lottery identifies effects for lottery-applicant families; twin births identify effects for families on the margin of another child.

MDVE: recode three arms as “coddle” versus “arrest”

Variable Code name Equals 1 when…
Instrument \(Z\) z_coddle form said advise/separate
Treatment \(D\) d_coddle officer advised/separated
Outcome \(Y\) (published) repeat violence occurred

One binary instrument and one binary treatment fit the standard IV formula.

“Coddle” is the shorthand for the two non-arrest responses. Collapsing three arms into two maps the experiment onto the Wald ratio. The clean data file has assignment and delivery only; the repeat-violence rates come from the published study.

Assignment to coddle raised actual coddling by 79 points

Share actually coddled
Form said coddle (\(Z = 1\)) 0.797
Form said arrest (\(Z = 0\)) 0.011
First stage \(\phi\) (difference) 0.786

A first stage this large means the random form had real power over what officers did.

Read: 79.7% of coddle-assigned cases were coddled; 1.1% of arrest-assigned cases were coddled. The difference, 0.786, is also the share of compliers. Robust SE from the regression on the next slide: 0.029.

One pf.feols call estimates the first stage and its strength

!pip install -q pyfixest                         # pyfixest is not preinstalled in Colab
import pandas as pd                              # pandas → load tables
import pyfixest as pf                            # pyfixest → regressions with robust SEs

GITHUB_DATA_URL = "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cmg777/intro2causal/main/data/"
mdve = pd.read_csv(GITHUB_DATA_URL + "ch3/mdve_clean.csv")   # 314 MDVE cases

mdve["z_coddle"] = (mdve["assigned"] != "Arrest").astype(int)    # Z: form said coddle
mdve["d_coddle"] = (mdve["delivered"] != "Arrest").astype(int)   # D: officer coddled

fs = pf.feols("d_coddle ~ z_coddle", data=mdve, vcov="hetero")   # first stage, robust SEs
fs.summary()                  # z_coddle 0.786 (SE 0.029), t ≈ 27 → F = t² ≈ 727

↗ Try it in Colab

vcov="hetero" gives heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors. With a single instrument, the first-stage F-statistic equals the squared t-statistic: 26.97² ≈ 727, far above the rule-of-thumb threshold of 10. The intercept, 0.011, is the coddling rate among arrest-assigned cases.

Coddling raises repeat violence by 14.5 points for compliers

Step Value
First stage \(\phi\) (from the data) 0.786
Reduced form \(\rho\) (published rates: 0.211 − 0.097) 0.114
LATE \(= \rho / \phi\) 0.145
Naive gap by treatment received 0.087

IV finds a bigger harm from coddling than the naive 8.7-point gap — selection bias was hiding it.

Being assigned to coddle raised the repeat-violence rate from 9.7% to 21.1% (ITT = 11.4 points). Only 78.6% of cases were moved by the form, so the effect of actually coddling compliers is 0.114 / 0.786 ≈ 0.145. The naive comparison of delivered treatments (8.7 points) is pulled toward zero because officers arrested the most dangerous suspects, making arrest look less effective.

The Wald ratio is a single division

import pandas as pd                              # pandas → load tables
# every course dataset lives in the repo's data/ folder on GitHub
GITHUB_DATA_URL = "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cmg777/intro2causal/main/data/"
mdve = pd.read_csv(GITHUB_DATA_URL + "ch3/mdve_clean.csv")   # 314 MDVE cases

z = mdve["assigned"] != "Arrest"                 # Z: form said coddle (True/False)
d = mdve["delivered"] != "Arrest"                # D: officer actually coddled

phi = d[z].mean() - d[~z].mean()                 # first stage: 0.797 − 0.011 = 0.786
rho = 0.211 - 0.097                              # reduced form: published repeat-violence rates
print(rho / phi)                                 # LATE ≈ 0.145 → +14.5 points for compliers

↗ Try it in Colab

d[z] keeps the coddle-assigned cases; d[~z] keeps the arrest-assigned ones; .mean() of a True/False column is a share. The recidivism outcome is not in the clean file, so the reduced form uses the published rates — the code still shows every step of the Wald ratio.

KIPP Lynn’s oversubscribed lottery created a ready-made instrument

  • KIPP: charter schools with long days and a “no excuses” culture
  • KIPP Lynn (Massachusetts) had more applicants than seats after 2005 → seats by lottery
  • \(Z\) = won the lottery · \(D\) = attended KIPP · \(Y\) = math test score

Validity check: lottery winners and losers looked alike at baseline — the balance check passes.

A school lottery is an experiment with built-in non-compliance: some winners decline, some losers get in later.

Independence comes from the lottery. Relevance will show up as a strong first stage. Exclusion requires that winning affects scores only through attending — the “motivated parents” side channel is the worry. The KIPP data are not public, so these numbers come from the published study.

Winning the lottery raised math scores by 0.355 standard deviations

IV component Estimate
First stage: lottery win → attended KIPP 0.741
Reduced form: lottery win → math score +0.355 SD
LATE: attending KIPP → math score +0.48 SD

Dividing 0.355 by 0.741 converts the effect of winning into the effect of attending.

74.1% of winners attended (first stage). The ITT of winning is +0.355 SD. LATE = 0.355 / 0.741 ≈ 0.48 SD. This applies to lottery compliers — families who attend if they win and do not if they lose.

One year at KIPP, for lottery compliers

+0.48 SD

in math — about half a standard deviation in a single year

A half-standard-deviation gain in one year is very large by education-research standards. Supporters cite it as evidence that structured programs can close achievement gaps; critics note it applies to motivated families who applied — the compliers — and may not generalize to all students.

Children from bigger families get less schooling, but is that causal?

  • Quantity–quality story: more children → less parental investment per child
  • OLS: each extra sibling goes with −0.15 years of schooling
  • Confounder: less-educated parents tend to have more children and less-educated children

The naive slope may just be telling us about the parents, not about family size.

Policy stakes: smaller-family policies (China’s one-child policy, forced sterilization programs in India) were partly justified by the quantity–quality trade-off. We need variation in family size that is unrelated to parents’ background.

Two accidents of birth shift family size almost at random

Twins at the second birth

  • An unplanned extra child
  • First stage: +0.32 children

Same-sex first two children

  • Parents try again for a mix of boys and girls
  • First stage: +0.08 children

Both instruments move family size for reasons unrelated to how educated the parents are.

Twins add a child essentially by chance. Sex composition works through a preference for having both a son and a daughter. Both first stages are positive; the twins instrument is much stronger.

The reduced forms are near zero, and 2SLS flips the sign

Estimate Effect of one more sibling on schooling
OLS (naive) −0.15 years
Reduced form, each instrument about zero
2SLS, both instruments +0.24 (SE 0.13)

Once family size changes by chance, the education penalty disappears — the OLS slope was selection bias.

If twins or same-sex siblings barely change children’s schooling (reduced form ≈ 0), then family size cannot have a big causal effect, whatever the first stage. The 2SLS estimate combining both instruments is +0.24 with standard error 0.13 — positive, not negative. Less-educated parents have more children and less-educated children, but one does not cause the other.

2SLS does the IV job in two regressions

Stage 1 — predict treatment from the instrument:

\[D_i = \alpha_1 + \phi Z_i + \gamma_1 X_i + e_{1i}\]

Stage 2 — regress the outcome on the predicted treatment:

\[Y_i = \alpha_2 + \lambda_{2SLS} \hat{D}_i + \gamma_2 X_i + e_{2i}\]

\(\hat{D}_i\) keeps only the part of treatment driven by the instrument; \(X_i\) are optional controls.

With one binary instrument and no controls, 2SLS reproduces the Wald ratio exactly.

2SLS generalizes ρ/φ to several instruments (twins and same-sex together), controls, and non-binary treatments. Weather-forecast analogy: stage 1 builds the forecast; stage 2 plans around the forecast, filtering out the noise from people’s own choices.

Hand-rolled 2SLS gets the coefficient right but the standard errors wrong

  • Running two separate feols calls by hand: right \(\hat\lambda\), wrong SE
  • The second stage treats \(\hat{D}_i\) as data, ignoring that it was estimated
  • Dedicated IV software corrects this automatically

Always run 2SLS as one IV call: pf.feols("y ~ 1 | d ~ z").

pyfixest’s formula has three parts: outcome ~ exogenous controls | endogenous treatment ~ instrument(s). The 1 means “intercept only, no controls”. Add controls before the pipe and extra instruments after the second tilde (e.g. d ~ twins + samesex).

pf.feols runs 2SLS in one formula and beats OLS on confounded data

!pip install -q pyfixest                             # pyfixest is not preinstalled in Colab
import numpy as np, pandas as pd, pyfixest as pf     # simulate · tabulate · regress

rng, n = np.random.default_rng(42), 10_000           # fixed seed → same numbers every run
df = pd.DataFrame({"u": rng.normal(size=n),          # u: hidden confounder (motivation)
                   "z": rng.integers(0, 2, size=n)}) # z: coin-flip instrument (a lottery)
df["d"] = (1.5*df.z + df.u + rng.normal(size=n) > 0.75).astype(int)  # d: nudged by z, chosen on u
df["y"] = 0.5*df.d + df.u + rng.normal(size=n)       # y: the TRUE effect of d is 0.5

print(pf.feols("y ~ d", data=df, vcov="hetero").coef()["d"])          # OLS ≈ 1.48: biased by u
print(pf.feols("y ~ 1 | d ~ z", data=df, vcov="hetero").coef()["d"])  # 2SLS ≈ 0.48: near the truth

↗ Try it in Colab

The chapter’s KIPP and family-size data are not public, so this block simulates a dataset where we know the truth (0.5). People with high u both take the treatment more and score higher, so OLS (≈ 1.48) badly overstates the effect. 2SLS uses only the lottery-driven variation and lands at ≈ 0.48 (robust SE ≈ 0.07). Chapter 6 runs the same syntax on real quarter-of-birth data.

A weak first stage makes IV unreliable: check that \(F > 10\)

  • Weak instrument: \(Z\) barely moves \(D\), so we divide by a number near zero
  • Consequences: estimates biased toward OLS, misleading confidence intervals
  • Rule of thumb: first-stage \(F\)-statistic above 10

MDVE first stage: \(t \approx 27\), so \(F = t^2 \approx 727\) — nowhere near weak.

Check the first stage before trusting any IV estimate.

Ship-and-rudder analogy: no matter how big the ship (sample), a tiny rudder (instrument) cannot reliably steer it. Also look at the reduced form: if the instrument’s effect on the outcome is invisible, the IV estimate is unlikely to be informative.

Pick the design that matches the variation you have

RCT (Chapter 1) OLS with controls IV / 2SLS
Needs Random assignment of treatment Confounders all observed A valid instrument
Handles All confounders Only observed confounders Unobserved confounders
Estimates ATE (everyone) Conditional association LATE (compliers)
Weakness Costly, often impractical Fails with hidden confounders Needs a strong, valid instrument

IV trades breadth for credibility: a clean answer, but only for the compliers.

Connections: an RCT with non-compliance is an IV problem — the ITT is the reduced form, and dividing by the compliance rate gives LATE (recall Oregon in Chapter 1). A fuzzy regression discontinuity (Chapter 4) is also IV, with the cutoff as the instrument.

The Resolution

Act III

The tempting mistake: “IV recovers the effect for everyone”

Objection. The lottery is random, so the IV estimate is the average effect of treatment in the whole population.

Response. IV only sees people whose treatment the instrument changed. KIPP’s +0.48 SD is the effect for lottery compliers; always-takers and never-takers may respond quite differently.

Different instruments identify effects for different groups: the twins instrument speaks for families nudged into a third child by chance, the same-sex instrument for families who wanted a mix of sons and daughters. Ask “who are the compliers?” before extrapolating.

The tempting mistake: “A huge sample cures a weak instrument”

Objection. The first stage is small, but we have millions of observations — precision will take care of it.

Response. Weak-instrument bias does not shrink as the sample grows. With a first-stage \(F\) below 10, 2SLS can sit close to the biased OLS answer. The cure is a stronger instrument.

Example from the chapter’s glossary: if quarter of birth barely predicts schooling (F = 3), the IV estimate can be far off even with 300,000 observations.

Key takeaways

  • Comparing by treatment received brings back selection bias
  • LATE = reduced form ÷ first stage, for compliers only
  • A valid instrument is relevant, as good as random, and works through one channel
  • Run 2SLS with IV software (pf.feols) and check the first-stage F
  • KIPP: large math gains · family size: no harm to schooling

MDVE: coddling raised repeat violence by 14.5 points for compliers, versus a naive 8.7. KIPP: +0.48 SD in math per year for lottery compliers. Family size: OLS −0.15 years per sibling, but 2SLS +0.24 (SE 0.13) — the naive penalty was selection bias.

Recap with the chapter’s visual summary: the problem (non-compliance), the tool (instrument → first stage and reduced form → LATE), the conditions (relevance, independence, exclusion), and the evidence (MDVE, KIPP, family size).
