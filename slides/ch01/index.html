The Question

Act I

Does health insurance actually make people healthier?

The U.S. spends more on health care than any other rich country — yet millions of adults have no insurance.

If we gave them coverage, would their health improve, or only their health-care bills?

Open with the policy stakes. Insurance debates (e.g., the Affordable Care Act) turn on this exact question. Ask the room for a show of hands: who thinks insurance improves health? Keep their answers in mind — we will test the intuition against two real experiments.

In survey data, insured adults report better health

Group Average health (1 = poor, 5 = excellent)
Uninsured 3.66
Insured 3.98

National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) 2009 — married adults aged 26–59, \(n = 18{,}790\).

The insured look healthier. The question is whether insurance caused the gap.

This is the first, most natural piece of evidence anyone would look at. A gap of about 0.3 points on a 5-point scale is sizeable. Before celebrating, ask: who chooses to have insurance? That question drives the rest of the lecture.

Our route through the chapter

  • The question — why a simple comparison cannot settle cause and effect
  • The investigation — potential outcomes, selection bias, and regression on a dummy; why random assignment removes the bias; two landmark insurance experiments (RAND and Oregon)
  • The resolution — what the experiments teach, how to read standard errors, and the traps to avoid

Learning objectives: explain why naive comparisons fail; define potential outcomes, selection bias, average treatment effects; describe how random assignment eliminates selection bias; use regression on a dummy to compare means; interpret the RAND and Oregon experiments; understand standard errors and statistical significance.

Three ideas carry the whole chapter

Potential outcomes

Each person has a health level with insurance (\(Y_{1i}\)) and without it (\(Y_{0i}\)).

Selection bias

People who choose treatment differ from those who don’t — before any treatment.

Random assignment

A lottery decides who is treated, so the groups are alike on average.

The chapter is the story of how the third idea solves the problem the first two reveal.

Other vocabulary we will meet along the way: causal effect, confounder, balance check, standard error, t-statistic, statistical significance, moral hazard, intent-to-treat, clustered standard errors. Define them as they come up rather than all at once.

The Investigation

Act II

Every person has two potential outcomes, but we only ever see one

The two roads

  • \(Y_{1i}\) — person \(i\)’s health with insurance
  • \(Y_{0i}\) — the same person’s health without it

The causal effect

\[Y_{1i} - Y_{0i}\] Only one road is ever travelled, so one term is always missing.

A causal effect compares a person with themselves in another world — not with someone else.

Fork-in-the-road picture: you take one path and never learn where the other would have led. The unobserved outcome is called the counterfactual.

A naive comparison can even get the sign wrong

Anika Ben
Health without insurance (\(Y_{0i}\)) 3 5
Health with insurance (\(Y_{1i}\)) 4 5
Buys insurance? (\(D_i\)) Yes No
Observed health 4 5
True effect +1 0

Insured Anika (4) looks worse than uninsured Ben (5), yet insurance helped her by +1.

Two hypothetical students. Anika is prone to illness and buys insurance; Ben is naturally robust and skips it. Naive comparison: 4 − 5 = −1, suggesting insurance harms health. The truth for Anika is +1. The comparison is polluted by the fact that Ben started out healthier.

Any observed gap = causal effect + selection bias

\[\text{Observed difference} \;=\; \kappa \;+\; \text{Selection bias}\]

\[\text{Selection bias} \;=\; \text{Avg}[Y_{0i} \mid D_i=1] \;-\; \text{Avg}[Y_{0i} \mid D_i=0]\]

The health gap that would exist between the two groups even if nobody had insurance.

Unless the bias term is zero, the observed gap does not equal the causal effect \(\kappa\).

κ (kappa) is the causal effect of insurance, assumed the same for everyone for simplicity. The selection-bias term compares the no-insurance health of people who did buy insurance with the no-insurance health of people who did not. In the NHIS, healthier, richer, better-educated people are more likely to be insured, so this term is likely positive.

Confounders open a back-door path from insurance to health

Because the same traits drive both insurance and health, the raw gap mixes their influence with any true effect.

A confounder is a variable that influences both treatment and outcome. The dashed arrow is what we want to measure; the solid arrows through the confounders are what contaminate the comparison.

We can never observe both roads for the same person

The individual effect \(Y_{1i} - Y_{0i}\) is unobservable — this is the fundamental problem of causal inference.

We cannot fix it with more data on the same people. We need a research design that makes the selection-bias term disappear.

Causal inference is the art of building a believable stand-in for the road not taken.

Every method in this course — randomized trials, regression, IV, RD, differences-in-differences — is a different strategy for constructing a credible counterfactual.

Regressing an outcome on a dummy reproduces the difference in means

\[Y_i = \alpha + \beta D_i + e_i\]

  • \(\alpha\) (intercept) = average \(Y\) when \(D_i = 0\)
  • \(\beta\) (slope) = treated mean − control mean
  • \(D_i\) is a dummy: 1 = insured, 0 = uninsured
  • In code: health ~ insurance

Regression on a dummy is just a comparison of means — plus a standard error for free.

Here Y is the health index (health) and D is the insurance dummy (insurance). The coefficient on the dummy is exactly the gap between group averages; regression adds a measure of precision.

A standard error tells us whether a gap is bigger than noise

  • Standard error (SE): how much an estimate would wobble across different samples
  • t-statistic = coefficient ÷ SE
  • Rule of thumb: \(|t| > 2\) → statistically significant at the 5% level
Purpose of the regression What we hope to see
Balance check small \(t\) — groups look alike
Treatment effect large \(t\) — evidence of an effect

Read every coefficient together with its standard error.

Bathroom-scale analogy: a good scale gives consistent readings (small SE), a cheap one jumps around (large SE). Throughout the chapter, standard errors appear in parentheses next to coefficients.

One pf.feols call gives the gap and its standard error

import pandas as pd                       # pandas → load tables

A coin flip breaks the link between treatment and personal traits

When a lottery — not the person — decides who is treated, choice can no longer bias the comparison.

A randomized controlled trial (RCT) draws both groups from the same population. Education, income, health habits — and traits we cannot even measure — have no say in who gets treated.

With enough people, randomization balances even what we cannot measure

  • Roll a die 10 times: the average may be far from 3.5
  • Roll it 10,000 times: the average is almost exactly 3.5

The law of large numbers does the same for randomized groups: their averages converge to the population average — for age, income, motivation, genes, everything.

Large randomized groups are alike on observed and unobserved traits.

Casino analogy: any single bet is a toss-up, but over thousands of plays the house edge always wins. The same averaging makes treatment and control groups comparable. With small samples, chance imbalances remain possible — another reason to check balance.

Under random assignment the selection-bias term vanishes

\[E[Y_{0i} \mid D_i = 1] = E[Y_{0i} \mid D_i = 0]\]

\[\Rightarrow\quad E[Y_i \mid D_i = 1] - E[Y_i \mid D_i = 0] = \kappa\]

Randomization makes the simple difference in means an unbiased estimate of the causal effect.

First line: with random assignment, the treated and control groups would have had the same average health without treatment. Plug that into the decomposition: the selection-bias term is zero, so the observed difference equals κ.

Controlled comparisons have a long history

When Who Contribution

Randomization went from a statistician’s idea to the gold standard for policy evaluation.

Lind’s theory (acids cure scurvy) was wrong, but his data were right — a lesson in letting evidence speak. An even older story describes a ten-day diet trial with a comparison group around 600 BCE.

Two questions: does cheaper care raise use — and does more care improve health?

The RAND Health Insurance Experiment ran from 1974 to 1982 at six U.S. sites. Families, not individuals, were randomized to plans. The catastrophic plan is the omitted reference group in every regression that follows.

A balance check is the same regression, run on a baseline trait

import pandas as pd                       # pandas → load tables
import pyfixest as pf                     # pyfixest → regressions with clustered SEs

+45%

45% more care bought no measurable improvement in health.

These outcomes were measured 3–5 years after random assignment. Every effect is small relative to the control mean and statistically insignificant. This is the RAND paradox: more care, not more health.

The null is precise: the data rule out large health gains

Free-plan effect on the general health index: \(-0.78\) with SE \(0.87\)

95% confidence interval \(\approx -0.78 \pm 2 \times 0.87 = [-2.5,\ 1.0]\) — on a control mean of 68.5

The experiment was big enough to find a meaningful effect; the effect simply wasn’t there.

A “precisely estimated null” differs from an underpowered study. Even the most optimistic end of the interval (+1.0 point on a ~68-point scale) is a tiny health gain. Lessons from RAND: people respond to prices; more care does not automatically mean better health; randomization exposed the NHIS gap as mostly selection bias.

Oregon’s 2008 lottery tested insurance for low-income adults

  • RAND studied middle-class families who all had at least catastrophic coverage
  • Oregon: about 75,000 low-income, uninsured adults applied to expanded Medicaid
  • About 30,000 were drawn by lottery to apply for coverage

A lottery for a public program created a new randomized experiment — for the people today’s debates are about.

Studied by Amy Finkelstein, Katherine Baicker and colleagues (2012, 2013). Today’s uninsured are younger, poorer and less educated than the RAND participants — would insurance help them more?

Only a quarter of winners enrolled, so we first measure intent-to-treat

  • Intent-to-treat (ITT): the effect of winning the lottery, whether or not the winner enrolled
  • Winning raised Medicaid enrollment by about 25.6 percentage points

\[\text{effect of insurance on enrollees} \approx \frac{\text{ITT}}{\text{effect of winning on enrollment}}\]

Scaling the ITT by the enrollment jump previews instrumental variables (Chapter 3).

Many winners failed to complete paperwork or turned out to be ineligible. Comparing winners with losers is still a clean randomized comparison — it just measures the effect of the offer. Dividing by the change in enrollment converts it into the effect of insurance itself.

Winning improved finances and mental health, not physical health

Medicaid enrollment +25.6 percentage points
Emergency-department visits +10% (a decrease was expected)
Self-reported health Modest improvement (+3.9 pp)
Physical health (cholesterol, blood pressure) No significant change
Mental health Improved
Catastrophic expenses and medical debt Decreased

Insurance protected wallets and moods; it did not move clinical measures.

Results from Finkelstein et al. (2012) and Baicker et al. (2013). Note the surprise on emergency visits: some policymakers expected coverage to shift care away from the ER; instead ER use rose.

Insurance’s clearest benefits may be financial protection and peace of mind.

Two very different populations, two different eras, the same physical-health finding. Oregon added the financial-protection and mental-health results.

Standard errors and confidence intervals measure sampling noise

Tool Symbol Plain meaning
Sample mean \(\bar{Y}\) The average in our data
Standard error \(SE(\bar{Y})\) How much \(\bar{Y}\) would vary across samples
t-statistic coef / SE How many SEs the estimate is from zero
95% confidence interval estimate \(\pm 2 \times SE\) Values consistent with the data

Any estimate from a sample would change with a new sample — inference quantifies by how much.

This is the toolkit we used implicitly all chapter: SEs in parentheses, |t| > 2 for significance, and the ±2 SE interval we used to show RAND’s null was precise.

Significance measures precision, not importance

  • A huge sample can make a tiny effect “significant”
  • A small sample can leave a large effect “insignificant”
  • Not significant ≠ no effect — it may just mean too little data

Always report the size of a coefficient and its precision.

Fire-alarm analogy: significance tells you the alarm went off, not how big the fire is. Look at the magnitude relative to a meaningful benchmark (e.g., the control mean) as well as the t-statistic.

The Resolution

Act III

The tempting mistake: “The insured are healthier, so insurance works”

Objection. Insured adults report better health, so coverage must improve health.

Response. The gap bundles any causal effect with selection bias — the insured were already older, richer and better educated. When RAND randomized coverage, the health advantage disappeared.

This is correlation-versus-causation in its most policy-relevant form. Recall the Anika/Ben example: the naive gap can even have the wrong sign.

The tempting mistake: “Not significant means no effect”

Objection. RAND’s health effects are insignificant, so maybe the study was just too small.

Response. Only if the estimate is imprecise. RAND’s confidence intervals are narrow and exclude large gains — a precisely estimated null, not a failure to look.

The distinction between “we found no effect” and “we found evidence of no large effect” is one of the most important habits in empirical work.

Key takeaways

  • Any comparison of groups = causal effect + selection bias
  • Random assignment sets selection bias to zero — then check balance
  • Regression on a dummy = difference in means + a standard error
  • RAND: free care raised spending 45% but did not improve health
  • Oregon: insurance brought financial protection and better mental health

Tie back to the opening show of hands: the intuition that insurance improves health is not wrong in every sense — it protects finances and mental health — but the physical-health effect in these experiments was small.

Recap with the chapter’s visual summary: the question (does insurance improve health?), the problem (selection bias), the solution (random assignment), and the evidence (RAND and Oregon).
