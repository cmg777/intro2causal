The Question

Act I

Does staying in school longer actually make you earn more?

College graduates earn roughly twice as much as high school graduates.

How much of that gap does school cause — and how much is about who chooses to stay in school?

Open with the stakes: families, students and governments spend enormous sums on education on the premise that it pays. Ask the room: if you could pay for one extra year of school for a random person, how much more would they earn? Keep the guesses on the board — we will test them with five different research designs.

In the 1980 Census, each extra year of school goes with higher pay

Average log weekly earnings by years of schooling — men born 1930–1939.

The gradient is steep and smooth. Is it a causal staircase, or a sorting of people by talent?

Each dot is the mean log weekly wage for one schooling level. Because the outcome is in logs, the slope reads as a percentage gain per year. The picture is striking, but it is only a correlation: people who stay in school longer may differ in ability, motivation and family background.

One regression puts a number on the gradient: about 7% per year

Learning objectives: estimate the simple OLS return and explain why it may overstate the causal effect; apply the OVB formula to ability; explain why an RCT is infeasible; use twin fixed effects; understand attenuation from measurement error; apply IV (twin reports, quarter of birth, compulsory schooling laws); use RD to test sheepskin effects; read a DD with a policy change; compare all estimates.

OLS is the starting point, and we suspect it is biased. Plus a synthetic scholarship “RCT” as the benchmark we cannot run in real life.

Three ideas carry the whole chapter

Return to schooling

\(\rho\) — the percentage gain in earnings caused by one more year of school.

Ability bias

Able people study longer and earn more, so OLS credits school for talent.

Measurement error

Misreported schooling dilutes the signal and drags estimates toward zero.

Ability pushes estimates up; noise pushes them down. The chapter is a tug-of-war between the two.

Other vocabulary as we go: omitted variables bias, bad control, twin fixed effects, reliability ratio, instrument, first stage, reduced form, Wald estimator, LATE and compliers, weak instruments, fuzzy RD, sheepskin effect, human capital vs signaling, parallel trends, convergence of evidence.

The Investigation

Act II

The simple model hides ability inside the error term

\[\ln W_i = \alpha + \rho\, S_i + e_i\]

\(\ln W_i\) is log weekly earnings (lnw), \(S_i\) years of schooling (s); ability, motivation and family background all sit in \(e_i\).

\[\hat\rho_{OLS} = \rho + \text{ability bias}\]

If \(S_i\) is correlated with what sits in \(e_i\), OLS measures the return plus a bias.

Because the outcome is in logs, ρ = 0.07 means each year raises earnings by about 7%. The key question is whether schooling is “as good as randomly assigned” with respect to everything in the error. It almost certainly is not.

Ability raises both schooling and earnings, so OLS likely runs high

\[\text{OVB} = \gamma \times \pi_1 > 0\]

  • \(\gamma\): ability’s effect on earnings
  • \(\pi_1\): how ability tracks schooling

Twist: if star talents drop out early to chase big opportunities, the bias could even turn negative.

The OVB formula from Chapter 2: the bias equals the effect of the omitted variable on the outcome (γ) times its relationship with the regressor (π₁). Both are plausibly positive, so OLS overstates ρ. The contrarian case: famous college dropouts who founded tech giants. They are rare, so the standard upward-bias view probably holds for most people — but the sign is an empirical question.

Age, gender and race are not ability — so ~11% per year still carries any ability bias.

When we can see ability, omitting it inflates the return by ~40%

Synthetic data (true return 0.09) Schooling coefficient (SE)
Short regression: omit ability 0.1229 (0.0024)
Long regression: include ability 0.0853 (0.0036)
True causal return 0.0900

In real data ability is invisible, so the long regression is a luxury we never get.

2,000 simulated people whose data-generating process we wrote, so we know the truth (~0.09, combining a direct effect and a channel through occupation). The short regression overshoots by roughly 40%; adding ability recovers the truth. The lesson: we need a design that removes ability by construction.

Controlling for occupation blocks a real channel of the return

Adding occupation shrinks the coefficient from 0.123 to 0.100 — not less bias, but a lost pathway.

Never control for an outcome of the treatment: it is a bad control.

Occupation is caused by schooling: education opens doors to better-paying jobs. Holding occupation fixed asks “what is the return among people in the same job?” — which removes one route through which school raises pay. Same synthetic data as the previous slide.

The ideal experiment — randomizing schooling — is off the table

  • Forcing some children to drop out is unethical
  • Assigning years of school by lottery for decades is impractical

But a lottery for a scholarship that nudges schooling is imaginable — and shows what we are aiming for.

We need designs that mimic random assignment without forcing anyone’s education.

Random assignment would break the link between ability and schooling, making the simple comparison causal — exactly as in Chapter 1. The scholarship idea previews the IV logic of the rest of the chapter: a random push to schooling.

A scholarship lottery recovers the true return with a Wald ratio

Synthetic scholarship lottery (true return 0.08) Estimate
Scholarship → schooling (first stage) 1.958 years
Scholarship → earnings (reduced form) 0.1605 log points
Per-year return (reduced form ÷ first stage) 0.0820

\[\hat\rho = \frac{\text{reduced form}}{\text{first stage}}\]

A random push to schooling, scaled by how much schooling moved, isolates the return per year.

2,000 simulated people; half randomly offered a scholarship that adds about 2 years of school. The offer is independent of ability and affects earnings only through schooling, so the ratio 0.1605 / 1.958 = 0.082 is close to the true 0.08. This is the Wald estimator of Chapter 3 — the template every quasi-experiment below tries to imitate.

Comparing identical twins cancels shared genes and upbringing

\[\Delta Y_f = \rho\, \Delta S_f + \Delta e_f\]

\(\Delta Y_f\) is the wage gap within twin pair \(f\) (dlwage); \(\Delta S_f\) the schooling gap (deduc). Anything both twins share drops out of the difference.

If ability is shared within a pair, differencing removes ability bias by construction.

Twinsburg data: 340 pairs of identical twins interviewed at the annual twins festival in Ohio. If one twin studied two more years than the other, their wage gap reflects schooling, not shared family or genes. No intercept: twins with the same schooling should have no systematic wage gap.

Within twin pairs, the return falls to about 6%

Method (Twinsburg twins) Return to schooling (SE)
OLS with controls (levels) 0.110 (0.010)
Twin fixed effects (differences) 0.062 (0.020)

Removing shared ability nearly halves the estimate — but is lower really closer to the truth?

The natural reading is “ability bias was inflating OLS”. Hold that thought: differencing also changes how much noise is in the schooling measure. The next slides show why the twin estimate can be too low.

Twin fixed effects is one regression on within-pair differences

!pip install -q pyfixest                  # pyfixest is not preinstalled in Colab
import pandas as pd                       # pandas → load tables
import pyfixest as pf                     # pyfixest → regressions with robust SEs

GITHUB_DATA_URL = "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cmg777/intro2causal/main/data/"
twins = pd.read_csv(GITHUB_DATA_URL + "ch6/twins_clean.csv")   # Twinsburg twins

first = twins[twins["first"] == 1]        # keep one row per pair (no double counting)
# wage gap on schooling gap; "- 1" drops the intercept (equal school → no gap)
fe = pf.feols("dlwage ~ deduc - 1", data=first, vcov="hetero")
fe.summary()                              # deduc: 0.0617 (0.0198) → ~6%

↗ Try it in Colab

Each pair appears twice in the file (once from each twin’s perspective), so first == 1 keeps one row per pair: 340 observations. The differences are pre-computed (dlwage, deduc). The measurement-error fix on the next slides is one line away: pf.feols(“dlwage ~ 0 | deduc ~ deduct”, data=first[[“dlwage”,“deduc”,“deduct”]].dropna(), vcov=“hetero”).

Differencing shrinks the signal but not the noise

\[\hat\rho_{FE} \approx \rho \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{Var}(\Delta S^*_f)}{\text{Var}(\Delta S^*_f) + \text{Var}(\Delta m_f)}}_{\text{reliability ratio}}\]

\(\Delta S^*_f\): true schooling gap within a pair · \(\Delta m_f\): gap in reporting errors.

Twins have similar schooling, so errors dominate — a reliability ratio of 0.5 would halve the estimate.

Bathroom-scale analogy: a scale that is off by a few pounds either way is fine for your weight, but useless for measuring the half-pound you gained today — the difference between two readings is mostly error. Same here: true schooling gaps within pairs are small, while misreports of a year or so stay the same size. Attenuation bias pushes twin FE toward zero.

Using the co-twin’s report as an instrument restores ~11%

Method (Twinsburg twins) Return to schooling (SE)
OLS (levels) 0.110 (0.010)
Twin FE (differences) 0.062 (0.020)
IV with co-twin’s report (levels) 0.116 (0.011)
IV with co-twin’s report (differences) 0.108 (0.034)

An independent second measurement filters out the noise — and the return climbs back to ~11%.

Each twin also reported the other’s schooling. That report tracks true schooling but has its own, independent error, so it is a valid instrument for own-reported schooling. pyfixest syntax: | educ ~ educt_t (levels) or | deduc ~ deduct (differences). Once noise is corrected, the within-pair return is close to OLS — suggesting ability bias in OLS is modest here.

Twins solve one problem but leave two caveats

Who is in the sample?

Twins who attend a festival in Ohio are self-selected — they may not represent all workers.

Are the errors independent?

If twins agree on a wrong answer, their reports share errors and the IV fix weakens.

Twin designs are clever but fragile; we need variation in schooling that is precisely measured.

External validity and the independence of measurement errors are both untestable here. The next strategy uses Census data where schooling is recorded for hundreds of thousands of men and the source of variation has nothing to do with ability: the calendar.

Birth timing plus dropout laws keeps Q4 babies in school longer

1980 Census: 329,509 men born 1930–1939 (Angrist and Krueger, 1991). Instrument: born in the fourth quarter (q4).

Birth quarter is unrelated to ability, but it shifts how much school a would-be dropout completes.

School-entry rules use a birthday cutoff, so children born late in the year start school slightly younger. Compulsory attendance lets students leave at 16 — and a Q4 child reaches 16 having completed a bit more school. Exclusion restriction: birth quarter affects earnings only through schooling.

Q4 births get slightly more school — and slightly higher pay

Filled dots = Q4 births, hollow = Q1 births. Left: years of education; right: log weekly earnings.

The same saw-tooth appears in both panels: the first stage and the reduced form line up.

Within each birth year, the filled Q4 dots tend to sit above the hollow Q1 dots — in schooling (first stage) and, more noisily, in earnings (reduced form). The upward trend in schooling across cohorts is absorbed by comparing quarters within a year.

Dividing the reduced form by the first stage gives about 7%

Step Estimate (SE)
Reduced form: Q4 → log earnings 0.0068 (0.0027)
First stage: Q4 → years of school 0.0921 (0.0132)
Wald ratio (reduced form ÷ first stage) 0.0740
2SLS 0.0740 (0.0280)

A tenth of a year of extra school, worth 0.7% more pay, implies a return of about 7% per year.

The instrument is strong: first-stage F ≈ 48, far above the rule-of-thumb 10. The IV estimate (7.4%) is close to OLS (7.1%) but far less precise (SE 0.028 vs 0.0004) — the price of using only the variation the instrument creates.

Three regressions and a ratio reproduce the birth-quarter IV

!pip install -q pyfixest                  # pyfixest is not preinstalled in Colab
import pandas as pd                       # pandas → load tables
import pyfixest as pf                     # pyfixest → OLS and IV/2SLS

GITHUB_DATA_URL = "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cmg777/intro2causal/main/data/"
qob = pd.read_csv(GITHUB_DATA_URL + "ch6/qob_clean.csv")   # 1980 Census, men born 1930–39

fs = pf.feols("s ~ q4", data=qob, vcov="hetero")     # first stage: Q4 → schooling (0.092)
rf = pf.feols("lnw ~ q4", data=qob, vcov="hetero")   # reduced form: Q4 → earnings (0.0068)
print(rf.coef()["q4"] / fs.coef()["q4"])             # Wald ratio = 0.074
iv = pf.feols("lnw ~ 1 | s ~ q4", data=qob, vcov="hetero")   # 2SLS: s instrumented by q4
iv.summary()                              # s: 0.0740 (0.0280) — same as the Wald ratio

↗ Try it in Colab

The IV formula reads: outcome ~ exogenous controls | endogenous ~ instrument. With one binary instrument and no controls, 2SLS equals the Wald ratio exactly; 2SLS also gives the correct standard error. First-stage F for a single instrument = t² of q4 in the first stage ≈ 48.

The IV return applies to students at the dropout margin

Compliers

Would-be dropouts whose schooling was changed by their birth quarter — the LATE is their return.

Not compliers

College-bound students and very early leavers: their schooling does not depend on birth quarter.

7% is the return for students on the edge of leaving school — not necessarily for future graduates.

Local average treatment effect (Chapter 3). Always-takers (would stay regardless) and never-takers (leave regardless of the rules) contribute nothing to the estimate. The return for other groups could be higher or lower — heterogeneous effects.

State laws on school-entry age provide a second set of instruments

\[S_{scy} = \phi_1 CL7_{sc} + \phi_2 CL8_{sc} + \phi_3 CL9_{sc} + \beta_s + \gamma_c + \delta_y + v_{scy}\]

\(CL7, CL8, CL9\): state required school entry by age 7, 8 or 9 (cl7, cl8, cl9) · \(\beta_s\) state of birth · \(\gamma_c\) birth cohort · \(\delta_y\) census year — all absorbed as fixed effects.

Laws differ across states and cohorts, so a child’s required schooling depends on where and when they were born.

Acemoglu and Angrist: data collapsed to state-of-birth × year-of-birth × census-year cells (about 2,400 cells), weighted by cell size, with standard errors clustered by state. The second stage regresses log weekly wages on predicted schooling with the same fixed effects.

The child-labor-law IV doubles the return: 13% vs 7%

Method (state, cohort, census-year FE) Return to schooling (SE)
OLS 0.0698 (0.0066)
IV / 2SLS (entry-age laws as instruments) 0.1288 (0.0371)

The instrumented return is larger than OLS — the opposite of what pure ability bias predicts.

One reading: the compliers here (children pushed into school by the laws) may have unusually high returns. Another: measurement error was biasing OLS down. But the standard error is large — the 95% interval runs from roughly 5% to 20% — and the next slide explains why we should be cautious.

A first-stage F of 6 signals weak instruments

First stage: instrument → years of school Coefficient (SE)
Entry required by age 7 0.1690 (0.0674)
Entry required by age 8 0.1872 (0.0621)
Entry required by age 9 0.3973 (0.0984)

Below the rule-of-thumb of 10, IV estimates are imprecise and can be biased — read 13% with caution.

Each instrument is individually significant, but together they explain too little of the schooling variation once all the fixed effects are absorbed. Compare the birth-quarter first stage, F ≈ 48. Weak instruments widen confidence intervals and make the estimate sensitive to small violations.

Skills or the piece of paper? Two theories predict different jumps

Human capital

School builds skills. Students just above and below a pass mark have the same skills → no earnings jump.

Signaling

The diploma signals ability to employers. Holding it should pay → an earnings jump at the pass mark.

Texas last-chance exit exam (Clark and Martorell, 2014): 46 score bins around the passing line.

An RD at the exam cutoff tests the value of the credential itself — the sheepskin effect.

“Sheepskin” because diplomas were once printed on it. The running variable is the score relative to the pass mark (minscore, 0 = cutoff). Students one point either side are near-identical in skills but very different in their chance of getting a diploma. RD model: Y = α + ρD + f(score) + e, with a separate quartic in the score on each side of the cutoff.

Passing raises diploma receipt sharply; earnings barely notice

Left: share receiving a diploma. Right: average annual earnings. Dashed line = passing score.

A big jump on the left, almost none on the right: the credential changes hands, the paycheck doesn’t.

Diploma receipt jumps from about half to over 90% at the cutoff. Earnings rise smoothly with the score — higher-scoring students earn more because they have more skills — with no visible break at the line.

The diploma jumps ~42 points, but earnings move only ~$60

Jump at the passing score (quartic on each side) Estimate (SE)
Diploma receipt (share) 0.417 (0.011)
Annual earnings ($) 62 (284)
Earnings level just below the cutoff ($) ~14,000

The credential alone adds almost nothing — the premium comes from what students learn.

Re-run of the chapter’s RD fits: separate quartic polynomials in the score on each side, weighted by cell size (diploma) or person-years (earnings), robust SEs. Because passing raises diploma receipt by ~42 points rather than 100, this is a fuzzy RD; scaling the earnings jump by the diploma jump (62 / 0.417 ≈ $150) still gives a negligible, statistically insignificant credential effect. DD on earnings ÷ DD on schooling gives a return of about 8%

Two-way fixed-effects DD (state + year FE) Estimate (SE)
Reform → years of school (“first stage”) 0.5039 (0.0165)
Reform → log earnings (“reduced form”) 0.0393 (0.0055)

It is the same policy variation the child-labor-law IV uses — now read as before-vs-after.

DD model: Y_st = α + δ(Treated_s × Post_t) + β_s + γ_t + e_st, run twice — once for schooling and once for earnings. The ratio is a Wald estimator built from two DDs, valid if the reform affects earnings only through schooling. Synthetic data, so treat the 7.8% as an illustration, not a finding.

Two-way fixed effects and a ratio give the DD return

!pip install -q pyfixest                  # pyfixest is not preinstalled in Colab
import pandas as pd                       # pandas → load tables
import pyfixest as pf                     # pyfixest → two-way fixed-effects DD

GITHUB_DATA_URL = "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cmg777/intro2causal/main/data/"
did = pd.read_csv(GITHUB_DATA_URL + "ch6/synthetic_did.csv")   # 20 states × 20 years

# DD with state and year fixed effects; cluster SEs by state
s = pf.feols("avg_schooling ~ treat_post | state + year", data=did, vcov={"CRV1": "state"})

print(e.coef()["treat_post"] / s.coef()["treat_post"])   # implied return: 0.078

↗ Try it in Colab

| state + year absorbs state and year fixed effects, so treat_post’s coefficient is the DD. Clustering by state allows each state’s errors to be correlated over time. s.coef() ≈ 0.504 years, e.coef() ≈ 0.039 log points.

The Resolution

Act III

The tempting mistake: “Twin FE is lowest, so it must be closest to the truth”

Objection. Differencing removes ability, so the smallest estimate (6%) is the cleanest.

Response. Differencing also magnifies reporting errors. Once the co-twin’s report corrects the noise, the within-pair return climbs back to about 11%. Lower is not the same as less biased.

Two biases pull in opposite directions: ability pushes OLS up, measurement error pushes twin FE down. A good empiricist asks which biases each design suffers from — not which number is smallest.

The tempting mistake: “No diploma jump means school does not pay”

Objection. At the Texas cutoff the diploma barely changes earnings, so education is worthless.

Response. The RD prices the paper, not the years. Earnings still rise steadily with skills, and the IV designs find 7%+ per year — school pays through learning, not the credential.

The sheepskin RD is evidence for human capital over signaling — not evidence against a return to schooling. Different designs answer different questions; read each one for what it identifies.

Key takeaways

  • Raw OLS (~7%) mixes the causal return with ability bias
  • Twins remove shared ability but magnify reporting errors (6%)
  • Instruments correct the noise and point to roughly 7–10%
  • The diploma itself is worth little; learning drives the premium
  • Trust convergence across designs with different weak spots

Tie back to the opening guesses. The causal return to a year of school is real and large — roughly 7–10% per year — and we believe it not because any one design is flawless, but because designs with different assumptions and different flaws land in the same neighbourhood.

Recap with the chapter’s visual summary: the question (does school cause higher pay?), the problem (ability bias, then measurement error), the five designs (twins, birth-quarter IV, child-labor-law IV, diploma RD, reform DD), and the synthesis (a convergent 7–10%).

When five imperfect designs agree, a year of school raises earnings by roughly 7–10%.
