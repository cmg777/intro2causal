The Question

[01:46] Host: The Question begins with a practical problem. People are free to respond to an assignment. A patient might decline medicine, a lottery winner might choose another school, or an officer might change a planned response. Why is that more than an administrative detail?

[02:02] Economist: Assignment describes the opportunity or instruction someone receives. Received treatment describes what actually happens. If those differ, an experiment still identifies the effect of assignment. But that effect need not equal the effect of receiving treatment. An offer that nobody accepts cannot tell us what attending the school would do.

[02:24] Host: My prediction is that we can rescue the experiment by keeping only people who followed instructions. They received exactly what researchers intended. Would that leave a cleaner comparison?

[02:34] Economist: That prediction fails because following instructions can be a choice related to outcomes. A family may accept a school offer because it expects unusually large benefits. An officer may reject advice and make an arrest because the incident seems dangerous. Removing those decisions from the data removes the protection supplied by random assignment.

[02:54] Host: So the naive comparison sorts people by what they received. It might compare students who enrolled with students who stayed away. What makes the resulting difference selection bias?

[03:06] Economist: Selection bias means the groups would have differed even under the same treatment. More motivated families might support learning regardless of school. More dangerous incidents might produce repeated violence regardless of police action. A difference between received-treatment groups mixes those preexisting differences with any actual treatment effect.

[03:27] Host: Could we fix that by controlling for everything we observe? We have already discussed regression as a way to compare people with similar recorded characteristics. Why introduce another method?

[03:38] Economist: Regression can help when the important confounding differences are observed and handled appropriately. But motivation, expectations, and incident severity can remain partly unmeasured. Instrumental variables uses a different route. It isolates a source of treatment variation that does not share those hidden differences, if its assumptions hold.

[03:58] Host: Let us build the vocabulary before calculating. What is the instrument, and how is it different from treatment or outcome? I would like to keep the school example attached to each term.

[04:11] Economist: The instrument is the outside influence we use to move treatment. In a school lottery, it is receiving an offer. Treatment is attending the school. The outcome is the later test score. Instrumental variables, shortened to IV, uses offer-driven attendance changes rather than all differences in attendance.

[04:36] Host: And the first stage sounds like an early step in that process. Does it describe the school improving scores, or the offer changing school attendance?

[04:46] Economist: The first stage measures how much the instrument changes treatment. Here, it compares attendance rates by offer status. The reduced form instead measures how much the instrument changes the outcome. Here, it compares later scores by offer status. Those comparisons both preserve the lottery; neither sorts students by their enrollment choices.

[05:09] Host: Then we will divide an outcome change by a treatment change. What should I call the answer? And why does the map introduce different kinds of people?

[05:19] Economist: The answer can be a local average treatment effect, shortened to LATE. Local means it describes people whose treatment changes because of this instrument. We call them compliers. Always-takers receive treatment either way, never-takers receive it neither way, and defiers respond in the opposite direction. We will unpack those possibilities carefully.

[05:43] Host: Let me recap The Question. An assignment can be random even when treatment received is not. Comparing actual recipients can bring hidden differences back. We need to keep the random influence and learn how strongly it changes treatment.

[05:58] Economist: Exactly. Remember the first distinction before remembering any abbreviation: what someone was offered, what they received, and what happened afterward. The first stage and reduced form connect those events. Their causal meaning depends on assumptions we are about to investigate.

The Investigation

[06:17] Host: The Investigation starts with non-compliance in Minneapolis. This is a serious setting: police responses to domestic violence. What decisions did the experiment study, and what did it leave outside its scope?

[06:31] Economist: Participating officers encountered eligible suspected misdemeanor domestic assaults. The experiment excluded severe or life-threatening injury cases. Both the suspect and victim had to be present. A randomly prepared report form instructed the officer to arrest, advise, or separate. Separation meant ordering the suspect away temporarily. Advice could include mediation.

[06:56] Host: So an arrest instruction was not the same thing as an arrest. I predict officers were more willing to override the gentler instructions when they thought someone remained at risk. Is that the pattern?

[07:08] Economist: That prediction matches the reported pattern. About 99 percent of arrest assignments led to arrest. The matching response occurred in about 78 percent of advice assignments and 73 percent of separation assignments. Officers sometimes arrested despite another instruction, including when a suspect became aggressive. That made actual response partly a selected decision.

[07:32] Host: The deviations matter even if they were sensible in the moment. Random assignment did not require officers to ignore danger. But the analysis must recognize that the cases moved into arrest could already have had worse prospects.

[07:46] Economist: Right. We should not confuse an imperfectly followed research protocol with a useless study. The random instruction remains available. The outcome was a later suspected domestic assault at the same address within six months. We will ask whether the instruction changed that outcome through its effect on police response.

[08:06] Host: Now for the tool: a causal chain reaction. We have an assignment, then an action, then an outcome. How does IV use that sequence without pretending that officers made random decisions?

[08:20] Economist: It begins by comparing everyone according to assignment. First, measure how much assignment changes the chance of receiving treatment. Next, measure how much assignment changes the outcome. If the instrument affects the outcome only by changing treatment, the second comparison runs through the first. Dividing separates the treatment effect from the strength of the nudge.

[08:44] Host: That division is sometimes called the Wald ratio. I want the direction clear. We divide the reduced form by the first stage. We do not divide by the share of people who happened to obey a particular instruction.

[08:57] Economist: Correct. The first stage is a difference in treatment rates across instrument groups. With no treatment recipients in the control group, that difference equals uptake in the offered group. With crossover in both directions, it does not. The subtraction matters because some people would receive treatment without the offer.

[09:18] Host: The map also mentions an older problem: separating supply from demand. How did that lead to this method?

[09:24] Economist: Philip Wright developed IV in work published in 1928. Market prices and quantities reflect both supply and demand moving together. A supply shifter that does not directly shift demand can help trace demand. That early insight has the same core logic: find an outside change with a defensible single channel.

[09:45] Host: What makes that channel defensible? A variable that predicts treatment is easy to find. Family income might predict school attendance very well, yet it also relates to test scores in many other ways.

[09:56] Economist: That brings us to identifying assumptions. Identification means connecting an observed comparison to the causal effect we want. Relevance comes first: the instrument must change treatment. A relationship with no real treatment movement cannot identify the effect. But relevance alone says nothing about whether the movement is causally useful.

[10:20] Host: Independence is the next condition. Does random assignment supply it automatically, or are there details we still need to check?

[10:29] Economist: Independence means the instrument is unrelated to the potential outcomes being compared, possibly after suitable controls. A properly run lottery supports that condition within its randomization groups. If different applicant groups face different winning chances, compare like groups or control for those groups. Checking baseline balance can reveal problems, but balanced observables do not prove independence.

[10:55] Host: And exclusion requires the offer to affect scores only through attendance. Suppose winning also changes what parents do outside school, even when the child never attends. That would be a second route.

[11:08] Economist: Yes. That hypothetical route would threaten exclusion if it changed outcomes outside the defined treatment. Random assignment of an offer does not eliminate that problem. It makes the offer effect credible, but calling the scaled result an attendance effect requires the additional channel assumption. No universal statistical test can establish exclusion.

[11:34] Host: Should we also worry about one student affecting another student, or different people receiving very different versions of treatment? The school experience could involve many ingredients.

[11:45] Economist: We need a clear treatment definition and a stable comparison. If one applicant's offer changes another applicant's learning through an unmodeled spillover, the simple chain needs more work. A school attendance effect can include its normal curriculum and environment. It does not separately identify the contribution of each ingredient in that school package.

[12:10] Host: Who LATE speaks for is the next question. A complier sounds like someone we can identify by checking whether they obeyed. Is the label really that simple?

[12:19] Economist: No. A complier is defined by two possible responses to the instrument. The child would attend when offered a seat and would not attend without one. We only observe one of those possibilities for any child. Therefore, observing a winner enroll does not reveal whether that child is a complier or an always-taker.

[12:40] Host: Let me try the other types. An always-taker would find a place whether the lottery offers one or not. A never-taker would stay away either way. What exactly is a defier?

[12:53] Economist: A defier would attend without an offer but stay away because an offer arrived. The monotonicity assumption rules out that opposite response. It allows the instrument to leave some people unchanged. It requires that, when the instrument changes treatment, it pushes in a common direction. It is about counterfactual responses, not moral character.

[13:17] Host: Could observed crossovers prove that defiers exist? A losing applicant who enrolls looks as if they are doing the opposite of assignment.

[13:25] Economist: That person could instead be an always-taker. To be a defier, they would also have to refuse attendance if offered a seat. We cannot observe that alternative. Crossover patterns can inform our judgment, but they cannot reveal everyone's type. Under independence and monotonicity, the first-stage treatment difference gives the complier share for a binary treatment.

[13:49] Host: Why does the ratio single out compliers rather than everyone who attends? I understand that the instrument moves them, but how do the unchanged groups disappear?

[14:00] Economist: Always-takers and never-takers receive the same treatment under either assignment. With exclusion, their outcomes therefore do not change because of assignment. The reduced form comes from compliers' treatment changes. Dividing by their share turns that overall outcome change into their average treatment effect. Without monotonicity, opposite responses can spoil this simple interpretation.

[14:37] Economist: Here it is only the study's shorthand for advice or separation together. We will say non-arrest response as well. Reversing the treatment coding would reverse the sign, so keep this definition fixed.

[15:00] Host: Assignment to non-arrest increased actual non-arrest by about 79 percentage points. That sounds strong. What are the less rounded inputs before we examine repeat violence?

[25:52] Host: The inference toolkit now gives the computation a name: two-stage least squares. We have been dividing effects. What do the two regressions do, especially when controls or multiple instruments enter?

[26:05] Economist: In the first stage, predict treatment from the instruments and the chosen controls. In the second stage, relate the outcome to that predicted treatment, keeping the same controls. With one instrument, the result matches the covariate-adjusted reduced form divided by the adjusted first stage. Multiple instruments can combine useful treatment variation and improve precision when their causal interpretations are compatible.

[26:32] Host: So the predictions are not a machine learning goal in themselves. They select the part of treatment variation tied to the instruments, after accounting for the controls. What can go wrong with doing both regressions manually?

[26:47] Economist: The coefficient can be correct while the reported standard error is wrong. Ordinary second-stage regression treats the fitted treatment as if it were the observed treatment. Proper IV software calculates prediction errors using actual treatment, rather than fitted treatment alone. A prediction error is the observed outcome minus the model's prediction. The uncertainty calculation therefore differs from an ordinary second-stage regression. You do need dedicated IV software, such as the estimator used in the companion notebook.

[27:20] Host: What is a weak instrument? I hear people use small first stage as if it were a complete diagnosis. But a small treatment shift might be measured very precisely in a large study.

[27:31] Economist: Weakness concerns how clearly the data distinguish the treatment movement from noise, not merely its size in familiar units. The first-stage F-statistic summarizes evidence that the instruments predict treatment, conditional on controls. Below 10 is a traditional warning rule, especially in the chapter's discussion of many weak instruments. Above 10 is not a universal guarantee.

[27:58] Host: Why might collecting many weak instruments make the answer look reassuringly similar to ordinary regression? That sounds like agreement, which I usually welcome.

[28:09] Economist: Many weak instruments can overfit the first stage. In finite samples, this can pull two-stage estimates toward the ordinary regression result. Agreement then does not show that selection bias was absent. An alternative called limited information maximum likelihood is less vulnerable to this many-instrument bias. Comparing estimates and inspecting reduced forms can reveal fragility; no estimator repairs a false exclusion assumption.

[28:40] Host: Let me recap The Investigation. We retain the random influence, check its treatment movement, and scale its outcome effect. Police response, school attendance, and family size all use that reasoning, while the credible population and uncertainty differ.

[28:57] Economist: And choosing a design comes before choosing software. The method's name never replaces its assumptions.

The Resolution

[29:25] Host: The Resolution begins with a devil's advocate claim. Once we have a random instrument, surely the resulting treatment effect belongs to everyone. My prediction is that a larger sample will eventually make that generalization valid.

[29:40] Economist: That prediction confuses precision with population coverage. More observations can sharpen the local estimate without changing whose treatment responds. Always-takers and never-takers still supply no instrument-driven treatment changes. Extending the estimate to them requires additional assumptions or evidence. A precisely estimated complier effect remains a complier effect.

[30:06] Host: The second claim is that a huge sample cures a weak instrument. Our map warns against that reassurance. How should we state the warning without making sample size sound irrelevant?

[30:17] Economist: A large sample does not guarantee a strong design. With many weak instruments, finite-sample bias can remain serious. A valid instrument with a fixed, nonzero treatment effect can become more informative as the sample grows. So it is too strong to say that size can never help. Inspect the actual first-stage strength and the inference procedure rather than trusting the observation count.

[30:43] Host: A small reduced form also needs care. It might mean the treatment has little effect, or it might reflect a small first stage. We need both links before deciding what the ratio says.

[30:55] Economist: Exactly. Read the first stage and reduced form separately. If treatment barely moves, the ratio can be unstable and ordinary confidence intervals misleading. If treatment clearly moves but outcomes hardly respond, the evidence more directly challenges a large treatment effect in that setting. Even then, assess the uncertainty before declaring an exact zero.

[31:17] Host: Let us collect the takeaways in the map's order. Comparing treatment received can revive selection bias. The IV ratio divides the reduced form by the first stage. We also need relevance, independence, exclusion, and monotonicity for the familiar local interpretation.

[31:34] Economist: Use two-stage least squares through proper IV software, and examine the first-stage diagnostic. KIPP Lynn provided evidence of substantial math gains for lottery compliers. The family-size experiments challenged a strong negative causal account of schooling differences. Their conclusion is absence of convincing schooling harm in those comparisons, with uncertainty and local limits, rather than proof for all families.

[32:02] Host: The thesis is a useful sentence to carry away: a random nudge, scaled by how far it moves treatment, reveals the causal effect for those it moves. What condition should I mentally attach before repeating it?

[32:17] Economist: Attach the assumptions. The nudge must be independent of the underlying outcome differences, move treatment, and affect the outcome only through treatment. For the standard complier interpretation, it must not push some people the opposite way. That sentence describes a justified causal chain, not a shortcut from any convenient correlation.

[32:39] Host: To recap The Resolution, local is a description of who we learned about, not an apology for the study. Strong first stages and credible channels matter more than an impressive sample count. Honest uncertainty belongs in every practical conclusion.

[32:55] Economist: And a useful policy answer can still be local. We learn what changed for the people affected by a real offer or assignment. The next task is to ask whether those people and that treatment match the policy under consideration. Generalization is another question to investigate, not an automatic reward for obtaining a causal estimate.

Wrap-up

[33:18] Host: Return to our learning goals. I can now explain why received-treatment comparisons can restore selection bias. I can interpret the ratio and identify its complier population. And I can ask whether the instrument and the uncertainty calculation deserve trust.

[33:35] Economist: The core thesis returns: a random nudge, scaled by its treatment movement, identifies an average effect among those moved, when the assumptions hold.
