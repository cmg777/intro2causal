The Resolution

[32:23] Host: The Resolution begins with a devil's advocate. Elite graduates earn more, so elite college pays. That statement still sounds persuasive in everyday conversation. We should defend the comparison and its assumptions, rather than defend regression simply because it is technical.

[33:09] Host: Here is my prediction for the other extreme. If I add enough controls, I will eventually prove there is no private college effect. The coefficient might become tiny and statistically insignificant. Would that count as success, provided I keep all the available observations?

[33:25] Economist: No. A tiny estimate could reflect a good comparison, a bad control, or a poor specification. We must justify controls before celebrating where the coefficient ends up.

[33:50] Host: And a family's decision still depends on more than the average coefficient. Financial aid can change the actual tuition difference. Students can value subjects, location, friendships, or other opportunities. The study challenges a particular earnings claim. It does not remove personal judgment from choosing a university.

[34:11] Economist: That is the policy implication. Avoid treating an uncontrolled private graduate earnings gap as a guaranteed return on tuition spending. Also avoid declaring that every expensive college is a bad investment. The evidence concerns particular graduates, institutions, and earnings outcomes. A responsible recommendation states those limits and separates earnings from other benefits.

[34:36] Host: Our takeaways start with a fair comparison. Regression holds measured controls fixed and summarizes the remaining treatment contrast. It cannot promise that unmeasured differences disappear. That is the central distinction between fitting a model and identifying a causal effect.

[34:54] Economist: The bias formula adds a practical diagnostic. Ask how an omitted trait differs with treatment after the shared controls. Then ask how that trait predicts the outcome in the long model. Their product explains the short-to-long coefficient difference. Its signs suggest upward or downward distortion. Its interpretation still depends on the credibility of the longer comparison.

[35:19] Host: Stability across reasonable models gives us another check. If important measured background traits no longer change the estimate after application controls, confidence grows. But simply collecting many similar controls can create a false sense of security. We need meaningful challenges to the design, including missing traits that might behave differently.

[35:41] Economist: We also distinguish uncertainty from bias. A standard error quantifies sampling variation, not whether the controls remove selection. More observations can narrow uncertainty around a biased answer. A more complicated model can change precision in either direction. Report the estimate's uncertainty while separately explaining why its causal interpretation may be credible.

[36:05] Host: Then the thesis is that regression delivers causal effects only when the right pre-treatment controls make treatment as good as random. We also need actual overlap and a suitable model for the controlled comparison. Those qualifications give the thesis content. They tell us what to inspect before trusting the treatment coefficient.

[36:26] Economist: To recap The Resolution, private college's raw earnings premium does not establish its payoff. The relevant application controls largely remove it in the original evidence. A credible regression argument explains selection, modeling, uncertainty, and the limits of the population being compared.
