[07:32] Host: Now give the method its full name.

[07:35] Economist: Difference-in-differences compares the treated group's change with the control group's change. First find each group's outcome after treatment minus its outcome before treatment. Then subtract the control change from the treated change. We often shorten the method's name to DD. The result is a change relative to another change.

[07:58] Host: The first difference removes a permanent starting gap within each group. The second removes the change they would have shared. But that second step seems to assume exactly what we are trying to establish: how the treated group would have changed without treatment.

[08:13] Economist: That is the identifying assumption: parallel trends. Without treatment, the average changes would have matched over the period we compare. It is about the untreated alternative, not whether the observed paths remain parallel after an effective treatment. A successful treatment can make the observed paths diverge.

The Resolution

[25:50] Host: The Resolution starts with a devil's advocate. I predict that groups starting at very different levels must be unsuitable for any causal comparison. If Atlanta begins with fewer banks, surely subtracting changes just hides the fact that the places are different.

[26:08] Economist: The prediction confuses levels with changes. A stable starting gap can disappear when each group is compared with itself over time. The key issue is how outcomes would have changed without treatment. Different initial levels may prompt useful questions, but they do not by themselves refute parallel untreated trends.

[26:28] Host: Let me try the opposite objection. If fixed effects control for permanent differences and national shocks, perhaps nothing important remains uncontrolled. Could we simply run the regression, cluster the errors, and regard the coefficient as causal?

[26:44] Economist: That is the second tempting misconception. A new state-specific road-safety effort can change mortality exactly when the drinking law changes. [27:04] Host: So a convincing analysis should explain why the control change represents the treatment group's missing change. It should examine earlier outcomes and simultaneous policies. [27:23] Economist: Those are design questions worth asking before interpreting any coefficient. Earlier outcomes can challenge a proposed trend assumption. A clean-looking earlier record cannot rule out every later problem. Checking the relevant threats is more informative than treating a long list of regressions as a certificate of causal truth.
