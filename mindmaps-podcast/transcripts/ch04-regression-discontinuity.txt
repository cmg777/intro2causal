The Resolution

[27:28] Host: We have reached The Resolution, starting with the devil's advocate. My objection is that RD just controls for the running variable. Why give it a special name when regression already lets us add age or test scores?

[27:42] Economist: Because the source of identification differs. With ordinary controlled comparisons, we hope treated and untreated people can be compared at the same control values. In sharp RD, the rule prevents that overlap. We instead rely on continuity and extrapolate toward a boundary. How we describe the trend therefore matters directly to the causal claim. The special name reminds us of that special argument.

[28:10] Host: Here is my prediction for the last part. If another policy changes at the same birthday, a mortality jump alone cannot tell us which policy caused it. Even an extremely precise estimate would not solve that identification problem.

[28:24] Economist: Correct. Precision tells us about sampling uncertainty, not which explanation is right. We must investigate competing changes at the boundary. At 21, the alcohol interpretation gains support from the legal rule and the pattern across death causes. But a jump on its own is not proof. The evidence must support the assumption that other relevant influences remain smooth.

[28:49] Host: The accepted mind map also asks us to check sorting and manipulation. People cannot choose to age themselves across a birthday. But an admissions score might be adjusted. What would make that a serious threat?

[29:31] Host: That hypothetical is useful because it explains the danger without accusing the Boston applicants of manipulation. The actual question is whether the process makes close neighbors credible comparisons. Neither an eligibility rule nor a clean graph settles that by itself.

[29:48] Economist: Exactly. Another misconception is that a placebo must always equal zero in the sample. Random variation makes exact zeros unusual.
